Design Project · Darwin, Australia

Green Solutions for Darwin’s Heat

How can plants, shade and smarter surfaces keep people cooler in one of Australia’s hottest, most humid cities?

CourseAPSC 169 · UBC Okanagan
TeamTeam 01 · 5 members
My roleResearcher and Report Conductor
TimelineSept – Dec 2026

Two ways to read this

Plain language

Darwin is hot and humid almost all year. Concrete, asphalt and dark roofs soak up sunlight and release it as heat, which makes streets and buildings uncomfortable. We looked for “green” fixes — trees, planted roofs and walls — that cool people and buildings without any mechanical cooling.

For engineers

Problem: cut heat gain and improve pedestrian thermal comfort in a hot, humid, wet/dry tropical climate, where humidity limits evaporative cooling.

Models: heat relief R = H × fs × ΔWBGT; roof heat flow q = (Tsurface − Tinside) ÷ Rtotal; evapotranspiration cooling Q = E × Lv; and a seasonal water balance.

The story

Why — objectives

Cool Darwin without mechanical cooling, in a way that works through both the wet and dry seasons and serves the people who need shade most: bus-stop users, school children and aged-care residents.

How — process

We compared three options, chose green solutions, then built three virtual prototypes, each testing the riskiest assumption of the one before.

What — outcome

A city-scale green design: shaded bus-stop shelters, street trees, parks, a green mall, and green roofs and walls. In our models, a green roof plus façade cuts heat flow into a shelter by 74–82%.

How the design evolved

  1. Research

    We mapped how green roofs, walls and façades cool through shading, evapotranspiration and insulation — and what limits them in Darwin: plant choice and light, water and upkeep, and a hot, humid climate with wet and dry seasons.

  2. Compare & choose

    We compared three ideas against our design requirements and chose green solutions: they cool year-round without external power, so they don’t depend on sun visibility the way solar desiccant cooling does.

    OptionHow it coolsWeakness in DarwinVerdict
    Solar desiccantSolar panels power a desiccant dryer, then the dried air is cooledDepends on sun and electricity; efficiency varies through the yearNot chosen
    High albedoReflective paint and window films bounce sunlight awayDoesn’t reduce humidity; dirt in the wet season cuts performance; reflects heat toward pedestriansNot chosen
    Green solutionsShade, insulation and evapotranspiration from trees, green roofs and green wallsNeeds water and upkeep; humidity limits evaporative coolingChosen

  3. Prototype 1 — city-scale calculation model

    A spreadsheet model compared street trees, bus-stop shade, parks, and green walls and roofs on cooling, water use and cost, using 125 trees per km at 8 m spacing and 400 pedestrians an hour. Busy footpaths gave the most heat relief per dollar, and green roofs the best building energy saving per dollar. The riskiest assumption was how much shade and WBGT reduction trees really provide.

  4. Prototype 2 — the green heat shelter

    We zoomed in on one design: a shelter with a green roof, vine façade, drip irrigation and rainwater tank, for bus stops, schools and aged-care homes. Heat-flow and water-balance calculations tested the riskiest assumption — that the plants can survive and cool on water and upkeep a community can sustain. The roof plus façade cut heat flow by 74–82%, but the roof needs about 33–41 L a day in the dry season, and without water its own cut falls from 86–87% to 59%. A plant screening passed two native species and rejected butterfly pea as a local weed.

  5. Prototype 3 — a Darwin-style city in Blender

    We placed the shelters, street trees, a park, a green mall and green roofs and walls into a 3D model with matching before-and-after views, to test placement, shade and coverage at city scale. Limit: the full tree layout was too heavy to render, so shade values come from the spreadsheet, not the render.

What these results do and don’t show

Everything here is calculated or rendered, not measured on site. Water is the weak point: without it, the roof’s cooling drops sharply. The biggest risks we identified are structural load on roofs, a constant water supply, and the years young trees take to grow.
